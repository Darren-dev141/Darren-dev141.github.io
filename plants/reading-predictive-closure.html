Predictive Closure — What a JEPA Keeps

Levy, Lidayan & Goldberg — a feature earns its place by what it adds to a set's power to predict itself.

Reading
Full text, 24 pp.

The idea, in one sentence: a JEPA encoder retains the predictive closure — the feasible feature set, sized to its fixed entropy budget, whose current values carry the greatest mutual information about their own next values. Membership is a set property: a feature can be worth keeping because of what it lets the set predict about its partners, not because of anything it does alone.

The experiment that carries it: steering training toward reconstructing one image (C) — at the largest weight tried — raised the decoding of its never-targeted partner (D) from 61.3% to 85.5%, while the untouched competing pair fell. Features subsidize each other across the set, measurably. Caveats on the label: one curve in that result is figure-only, and the reconstruction steering rests on a single seed.

The local verdict, tested rather than assumed: the concept's machinery needs three things — a learned encoder, a fixed entropy budget, and a self-prediction objective over time. Our signal problems have none of the three, so the idea was run against our own labeled data anyway, as a set-versus-single comparison. Clean null: the set added nothing the best single signal didn't already say. The paper is planted for the definition and the discipline it teaches — check whether a representation can even express a feature before claiming it contains one — not for a transfer it didn't survive.
