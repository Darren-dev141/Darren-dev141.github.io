The Buyback

Make a decision system buy back its own past decisions — at prices set by the confidence it recorded at the time.

Maturity
🌳 Evergreen

Pilot complete

The situation, invented for the purpose: a decision system must repurchase its own past decisions at prices set by the confidence it originally recorded, while an adversarial re-judgment — the same questions, with the options' definitions swapped — decides which picks survive. Survivors pay out. Flippers burn their stake. A schema keeps its seat only while its picks survive.

The pilot ran on the census tape — 736 paired production decisions — with three predictions written down before the analysis. All three held, and the interesting one is the paradox: the most confident quartile of decisions flipped the most (74.5%, against 63.0% in the least confident quartile). Any gate that trusts high-confidence calls more is, on this tape, selecting for the wording artifacts. By schema, stability turned out to be a property a design can have: one schema flipped 85.9% of its picks; another, built differently, flipped only 18.1%.

The honest gaps

Two, printed on the label. First: the pilot's verdicts are the machine's — the human arm of the experiment couldn't run, because the operator's own outcome verdicts join to 0 of the 968 census items. His decisions about the decisions were never recorded; building that instrument is the stated next move. Second: the 74.2% figure came from pilot code. The stake accounting itself was later proven — see RUN-001 — but for a reference implementation; nobody has yet replayed the pilot against the proven ledger, and this page won't pretend otherwise.
