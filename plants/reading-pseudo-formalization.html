Pseudo-Formalization for Automatic Proof Verification

Barkallah, Bailey, Wen, Abouzaid & Ma — verify a proof the way you'd audit a building: block by block, anchored to the source.

The idea: don't ask a judge model "is this proof right?" — decompose the proof into small blocks, pseudo-formalize each block's premises and conclusion, verify the blocks, and then re-anchor the verdicts to the source text with a calibrator pass. On their main benchmark the full pipeline beats a plain LLM judge on recall at equal precision — 0.66 at 0.77, against 0.54 at 0.76.

The sobering numbers are also in the paper, which is why it's worth planting: on 100 real papers with 109 author-disclosed errors, both verifiers still miss about 40% of the known errors even at k=8 samples; the pipeline costs roughly 9–10× the baseline; and the authors' own audit found their judge had mislabeled 2 of 8 sampled "true positives." Ablations show the source-anchored calibrator is load-bearing — remove it and the method falls below the plain baseline.

Why it grows here: each block verdict is still one model call over natural-language premises — the same fragile atom the census measured at 85.8% flips under rewording, and the paper's faithfulness gate explicitly treats cosmetic rephrasing as safe. The paper never perturbs its own verifier, so the flip question is untested, not answered. What limits the damage is architecture: small blocks shrink the blast radius of any single bad call. That lesson — structure around a fragile judge instead of trusting it — transfers even where the method doesn't.
