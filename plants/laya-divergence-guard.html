Laya + Divergence Guard

A judge that argues with its operator — and the watcher built to catch it doing so.

Laya is a local decision server: callers hand it a typed schema — a choice among labeled options, yes/no questions, ordinal scores — and it returns a pick, a confidence, and a receipt in a production log. It gates real work: which job leads proceed, which claims check out, which bets carry an edge.

It is good at sounding like its operator. Measured against his recorded verdicts, it reproduces the calls he accepted almost exactly — and it also reproduces the calls he rejected, re-asserting the same side, confidently, weeks later, with no signal that it is diverging from him. Agreement with a person turns out to include agreeing with their mistakes at full confidence.

So a second, smaller system was built: the Divergence Guard. It keeps the operator's rejected calls on file, and every two minutes it checks new decisions against them. When Laya re-asserts a rejected side, the guard raises a flag and holds it until a human resolves it. On real history it raised 182 flags — every one of them held.

Why it isn't evergreen

The guard proves the divergence exists; it doesn't fix the judge. Laya's gates are currently off while a rebuilt version — with labels a wording-swap can't invert — works through a canary suite derived from the census. Until that version passes its own tests and earns the seat back, this plant stays budding: real, running, and not yet trusted.
